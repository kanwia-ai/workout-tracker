# Myth: Lifting converts fat into muscle

## The myth (verbatim)
"I'm turning my fat into muscle." "Lifting converts fat to lean tissue."

## Why the myth persists
The visual experience reinforces it: a beginner starts lifting, loses fat, gains muscle, and the same body part that used to be "soft" is now "firm." It feels like a conversion. The lay vocabulary doesn't easily distinguish between simultaneous fat loss and muscle gain vs. an actual substance conversion.

## What the research actually says
1. **Fat (adipose tissue) and muscle (skeletal muscle tissue) are biologically distinct.** Adipocytes store triglycerides; myocytes generate force via actin-myosin. There is no metabolic pathway that converts one to the other. Fat is mobilized via lipolysis and burned for fuel; muscle is built via protein synthesis from amino acids.
2. **Body recomposition is real** (Barakat et al. 2020 Strength Cond J review): novices, returning lifters, and overweight individuals can simultaneously lose fat AND gain muscle when resistance training + adequate protein + a modest deficit or maintenance calories are combined. But this is two parallel processes, not a conversion.
3. **For trained / lean individuals** (Slater 2019), simultaneous fat loss and muscle gain is much harder — you usually need a slight surplus to maximize hypertrophy. But recomp is still possible in some contexts.

## The corrected understanding
- You can simultaneously lose fat and gain muscle. They're parallel processes.
- "Converts fat to muscle" is biologically incorrect language for what is happening (and what's happening is already cool — give it the right name).
- Fat goes via lipolysis → fatty acid oxidation → CO2 + H2O. Muscle is built from dietary protein + mechanical stimulus.
