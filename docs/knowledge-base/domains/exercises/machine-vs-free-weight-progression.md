# Machines vs free weights — stepping stone, destination, or both

## Claim

The "free-weight superiority" assumption is **not supported by the research**. The Haugen 2023 meta-analysis pooled 13 controlled trials comparing free-weight vs machine resistance training and found:

- **Hypertrophy:** equivalent at matched volume.
- **Strength:** modality-specific — free-weight training transfers best to free-weight 1RM tests; machine training transfers best to machine 1RM tests. Each modality builds strength *in itself* equally well.
- **Jump performance:** small free-weight advantage in some studies, consistent with the higher CNS / stabilization demand of free-weight compound work.

**Practical interpretation:**

1. **Machines are not "for beginners only."** They produce equivalent hypertrophy stimulus. They are *tools*, not a tier.
2. **For hard-to-feel exercises, machines are the FIRST progression**, not the last resort. The trainer (philosophy doc §7): *"Sometimes a machine version first to teach the body the pattern."* This is research-supported (Calatayud 2016 on mind-muscle connection; Snyder 2009 on lat pulldown specifically) — fewer balance variables = lifter can focus on the target-muscle contraction.
3. **For older or returning lifters, machines reduce stabilization demand.** The Saeterbakken 2019 review found stability-demanding exercises produce more synergist / stabilizer work, less prime-mover loading. For someone whose prime mover is the limiting factor (returning from layoff, age-related strength loss), the machine *lets the prime mover work* without the stabilizer fatigue ceiling.
4. **For experienced lifters with goals beyond hypertrophy, free weights still have specific virtues.** Strength sport (powerlifting, Olympic lifting) is free-weight tested; transfer is modality-specific. Athletic performance (jumping, sprinting) benefits from the CNS demand of free-weight compounds.

The free-weight-bias-as-default is a **gym culture myth**, not a research conclusion. See `myth-machines-are-inferior`.

## Stepping stone vs destination

A machine can be either or both:

### Stepping stone (transient use, graduate to free-weight)

- **Lat pulldown machine → assisted pull-up → full pull-up.** See [lat-pulldown-cueing](lat-pulldown-cueing.md). The machine teaches the lat-driven pull pattern; the lifter graduates when the connection is consistent.
- **Smith machine squat → goblet squat → barbell back squat.** For users learning the squat pattern, the fixed bar path reduces the variables to one (depth + drive). When the lifter can squat the goblet variant for clean reps, the barbell back squat is the destination.
- **Hammer-strength chest press → dumbbell bench → barbell bench.** Same logic — stabilization demand increases stepwise.
- **Machine row → chest-supported DB row → barbell bent-over row.** Same.

### Destination (long-term use, no need to graduate)

- **Leg press / hack squat** for a user with chronic knee or back limits. There's no rule that says the user must graduate to a free-weight squat — leg press is a *complete* leg exercise and can be the permanent main lift for this person.
- **Cable lateral raise** for delt isolation. Cables provide more even tension through the ROM than dumbbells; many advanced lifters prefer cables permanently.
- **Pec deck / machine fly** for chest isolation. Stability is intentionally removed; the lifter focuses on pec contraction. Permanent tool in the toolbox.
- **Glute / hip abduction machines** for glute medius work (see [hard-to-feel-exercises-catalog](hard-to-feel-exercises-catalog.md)). Machines isolate the target with less compensatory cheating than band/floor variants for many users.

The user's goal, training age, injury status, and equipment access decide which framing applies. The engine should default to "machine + free-weight options exist, user chooses" — not "machine is a step toward free-weight."

## Nuance

- **Machine variants for hard-to-feel exercises are PREFERRED, not inferior.** The chest-supported machine row, the lat pulldown, the seated leg curl, the hip abduction machine — these are the *first* exercises a lifter should use for the target muscle in question, specifically because they remove the synergist / balance variables that cause the mind-muscle failure (see [hard-to-feel-exercises-catalog](hard-to-feel-exercises-catalog.md)).
- **"Stabilization demand is a feature" is true only when it's the GOAL.** For an athlete training jumping, the free-weight squat's CNS + stabilizer demand transfers to athletic performance. For an older lifter trying to keep their quads big, that same demand is just an obstacle to loading the quad.
- **Equipment access is a real constraint, not a choice.** A user with bands and dumbbells has no machine option. The engine respects equipment availability — but does NOT treat the lack of machines as a problem. A well-designed home program (dumbbells + bench + bands) produces equivalent results to a full gym at matched volume for hypertrophy goals.
- **The Schwanbeck 2009 Smith-machine-vs-free-weight squat study** found *small* EMG differences favoring free-weight quad activation. This is one EMG study, not a hypertrophy outcome study — and the Haugen 2023 meta on hypertrophy outcomes found no differences. Don't extrapolate EMG findings into "machines build less muscle."
- **Some machine designs are bad.** A poorly-fit machine (wrong height, wrong cam profile, broken cable) is worse than a free-weight equivalent. "Machines are not inferior" assumes the machine is appropriately fitted to the lifter.

## What this contradicts

- **"Machines are for beginners; real lifters use free weights."** Haugen 2023 meta: equivalent hypertrophy, equivalent strength on modality-specific tests. Wrong premise. Myth: `myth-machines-are-inferior`.
- **"You should always graduate from machines to free weights."** False as a universal rule. Some users have permanent reasons (injury, equipment, preference) to stay on machines. Some exercises (cable / machine isolations) have no free-weight equivalent that's better.
- **"Free weights are functional; machines are isolation."** Confused category. Functional refers to *transfer to real-world tasks*. Many machines transfer to function (leg press transfers to standing up from a chair; lat pulldown transfers to climbing). Many free-weight exercises don't transfer to anything except themselves (barbell curl is not particularly functional). See myth `functional-training-is-better`.

## Application in this app

- **Equipment filter in the variant pool**: the planner respects `equipment` profile. A user with `bands_only` doesn't see machine variants. A user with `full_gym` sees them as first-class options, not fallbacks.
- **`mind_muscle_felt='missed'` triggers machine-first regression** for hard-to-feel exercises (see [hard-to-feel-exercises-catalog](hard-to-feel-exercises-catalog.md)).
- **Training-age + injury → machine bias**:
  - `training_age <= 6 months` AND `injury_list` includes anything → prefer machine variants for the affected pattern (back-squat → leg press, conventional deadlift → trap-bar or RDL, bench press → machine press or chest-supported DB).
  - `age >= 50` (when surfaced in onboarding) AND first month of training → prefer machine + supported variants by default. Graduation to free-weight at user's option.
  - Returning from `>3-month layoff` → first 2 weeks bias to machine; reintroduce free-weight in week 3-4.
- **LLM nuance copy:**
  - "We're starting you on the lat pulldown because pull-ups demand full bodyweight from rep 1. The pulldown lets you build the back connection at any load."
  - "Leg press is your main quad lift this block — you have a knee flag, and the leg press loads the quad fully without spinal compression."
  - "Machines aren't a downgrade. The Haugen 2023 meta found equivalent muscle growth at matched volume — the lift you can do consistently with good form is the lift that grows muscle."
  - Do NOT say: "machines are easier so they build less muscle" (wrong); "you should graduate to free weights" (presumptuous and often wrong); "real lifters use barbells" (gym-bro doctrine).
