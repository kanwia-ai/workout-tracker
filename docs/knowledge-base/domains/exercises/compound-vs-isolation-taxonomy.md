# Compound vs isolation — what actually defines a compound

## Claim

A **compound** (multi-joint) exercise satisfies all four of the following:

1. **Multi-joint** — at least two joints articulate against load through the working ROM (e.g., back squat = hip + knee + ankle; bench press = shoulder + elbow; row = shoulder + elbow + scapula).
2. **Large muscle recruitment** — recruits one or more *large* prime movers (quad, glute, ham, pec, lat, back, delt) plus synergists that together represent the bulk of a body region's musculature.
3. **Load-scalable** — the lift accommodates a meaningful absolute-load progression (kilos / pounds added across mesocycles), not just rep escalation. Most compounds can be loaded to 1RM-relevant intensities.
4. **Transfers to a functional pattern** — the movement matches a basic locomotor / postural pattern (squat, hinge, push, pull, carry, lunge) that has real-world utility outside the gym.

An **isolation** (single-joint) exercise articulates against load at one joint, recruits a single prime mover (often a smaller muscle), and is most useful as a volume / lagging-muscle tool — not as the day's main lift.

**Canonical compounds** (this app's reference list):
- **Squat pattern:** back squat, front squat, goblet squat, hack squat, leg press (multi-joint despite seat-supported), Bulgarian split squat, walking lunge.
- **Hinge pattern:** conventional deadlift, sumo deadlift, trap-bar deadlift, Romanian deadlift, good morning, kettlebell swing (ballistic hinge).
- **Horizontal push:** bench press (barbell / dumbbell), push-up, dip (some flag as vertical), machine chest press (when seat is upright).
- **Vertical push:** overhead press (standing / seated barbell), dumbbell shoulder press, push press, landmine press.
- **Horizontal pull:** barbell bent-over row, single-arm DB row, T-bar row, inverted row. (Note: chest-supported and machine row variants are flagged separately — see [chest-supported-not-compound](chest-supported-not-compound.md).)
- **Vertical pull:** pull-up, chin-up, lat pulldown (also flagged hard-to-feel).
- **Glute compound:** barbell hip thrust (multi-joint hip + knee, large glute recruitment, load-scalable, transfers to running and jumping — see [hip-thrust-glute-priority](hip-thrust-glute-priority.md)).
- **Carry/loaded carry:** farmer's carry, suitcase carry (multi-joint stabilization).

**Canonical isolations:**
- Quad: leg extension (knee only).
- Ham: lying / seated leg curl (knee only).
- Glute med: hip abduction (machine or cable), banded clamshell, banded lateral walk.
- Calf: standing / seated calf raise (ankle only).
- Chest: cable / machine fly, pec deck (shoulder only — elbow nearly locked).
- Back: lat pullover (shoulder only when arms stay straight), straight-arm pulldown.
- Delts: lateral raise, front raise, rear delt fly / reverse fly (shoulder only).
- Biceps: dumbbell curl, hammer curl, cable curl, concentration curl (elbow only).
- Triceps: pushdown, overhead extension, kickback, skull crusher (elbow only).
- Forearms: wrist curl / extension (wrist only).
- Core: crunch, leg raise, side bend (spinal articulation only; planks are anti-movement isometric).

## Why compounds first

The Simão 2012 review and Nunes 2021 meta-analysis converge on **first-position bias**: the exercise performed first in a session improves more than the same exercise performed last. Three mechanisms:

1. **CNS readiness for high-threshold motor units.** Compounds at heavy loads require recruitment of the largest, fastest fibers. The CNS is at its freshest at session start; fine-motor isolation work later can still recruit local fibers because per-set systemic cost is lower.
2. **Motor-unit fatigue accumulates session-wide.** Even between non-overlapping muscle groups, central fatigue from heavy compounds reduces force output on subsequent lifts. The drop is asymmetric — heavy compound first means small drop on isolation later; isolation first means large drop on the compound that follows.
3. **Time efficiency.** A compound trains 3–6 muscles per set with cardiovascular + skill stimulus bundled in. For a 45-minute session, the compound earns its place by producing the most stimulus per minute.

The trainer's framing (philosophy doc §5): *"A compound recruits multiple joints and produces fatigue, strength, and growth that smaller exercises can't. Put them when the user is fresh; let them warm up everything downstream. This is categorical: no scenario where leading with a fly machine beats leading with a bench press."*

## Nuance

- **The "joint count" criterion alone is insufficient.** Leg press is multi-joint but seat-supported (no balance/spinal-stabilizer demand). It still counts as a compound for our purposes because it satisfies criteria 2 + 3 + 4. The chest-supported row is multi-joint but gravity is removed — this is the edge case that fails criterion 2's *functional recruitment* sense (see [chest-supported-not-compound](chest-supported-not-compound.md)).
- **A bicep curl is single-joint by anatomy but trains a small muscle alone — no question it's isolation.** A hip thrust is single-joint at the hip (knee largely static) but trains a huge muscle (glute max), is heavily load-scalable, and transfers to function — we treat it as a compound for ordering and priority. The *function* of the criteria, not their literal count, governs classification.
- **"Compound" is not a synonym for "free-weight" or "hard."** A leg press is a compound. A machine row (with chest-supported pad and no spinal demand) is closer to isolation in stimulus profile despite being multi-joint. The classification is about *what the exercise produces*, not *what equipment it uses*.
- **Compound ≠ "main lift."** A program can use a high-SFR machine compound (leg press, hack squat, neutral-grip DB press) as the session's main lift — particularly for older, returning, or injured lifters. Main-lift status is about position in the session and primacy of focus; compound is about the exercise's intrinsic characteristics.

## What this contradicts

- **"Only compounds build muscle; isolations are wasted reps."** False at matched volume. See [exercise-selection-compound-vs-isolation](../programming-fundamentals/exercise-selection-compound-vs-isolation.md). Myth: `myth-only-compounds-build-muscle`.
- **"All multi-joint exercises are compounds."** Chest-supported / fully-machine variants without significant stabilization or spinal loading have isolation-like stimulus profiles. See [chest-supported-not-compound](chest-supported-not-compound.md).
- **"Compounds are intrinsically better than machines."** Compound-ness is independent of equipment modality. See [machine-vs-free-weight-progression](machine-vs-free-weight-progression.md).

## Application in this app

- **`role` tag** on every variant in `src/lib/planner/variants.ts` is the source of truth: `'main lift' | 'accessory' | 'isolation' | 'core' | 'rehab' | 'mobility' | 'cardio'`. The engine orders sessions strictly by role priority — `main lift` first, then accessory, then isolation. The LLM may explain order but never reorder it.
- When classifying a *new* exercise (LLM-emitted or free-exercise-db import), evaluate against the four criteria above:
  - Multi-joint? ✓
  - Recruits a *large* prime mover (not just a small muscle)? ✓
  - Load-scalable to meaningful absolute load? ✓
  - Transfers to a basic locomotor / postural pattern? ✓
- If all four → compound. If three of four → likely compound accessory (chest-supported row, machine press, leg press for a returning lifter). If two or fewer → isolation, regardless of joint count.
- **LLM nuance copy**: when justifying *why* a given lift leads the session, cite the three mechanisms (CNS readiness, fatigue cascade, time efficiency) rather than vague appeals to "compound = best."
